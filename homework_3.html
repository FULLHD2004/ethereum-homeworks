How penetration scores behave as systems scale

Explore how n systems and m attackers shape random penetration scores. This experiment compares Bernoulli and Rademacher increments, traces individual outcomes, and checks the Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem against simulated data.

1. Problem setup and interpretation

Imagine that each of m independent attackers attempts to penetrate a collection of n systems in sequence. For attacker j and system i, let Xj,i be the random score obtained at that step. A trajectory is the cumulative sum of that attacker’s scores over the first k systems: Sj,k = Xj,1 + ... + Xj,k. Each attacker follows the same probability model and all increments are independent across both attackers and steps.

Under the Bernoulli model, a successful penetration scores 1 and an unsuccessful one scores 0, with success probability p. Under the Rademacher model, each step contributes +1 or −1 with probability 1/2 each: it is a symmetric net-score model, not a literal count of successful penetrations. The negative scores represent setbacks, defensive wins, or losses of score. The attacker population m gives us repeated sample paths; it does not change the distribution of a single attacker’s score.

5. Law of Large Numbers: why averages stabilize

The Weak Law of Large Numbers states that for IID increments with finite mean μ, the sample average Sn/n converges in probability to μ. This means that, for any fixed ε > 0, the probability of the average differing from μ by more than ε approaches zero as n increases. Here μ = p for Bernoulli and μ = 0 for Rademacher. The chart compares the simulated fraction of attackers whose averages are outside the selected tolerance with a rigorous theoretical upper bound from Chebyshev’s inequality (not an exact probability).

P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ² / (n ε²)   →   0

6. Variance: the mathematical effect of normalization

The purpose of this section is to investigate how the variability of attackers' penetration scores changes as the number of systems increases. Even when attackers follow the same probability model, they do not necessarily obtain the same score because their penetration attempts are random. Variance measures how much their scores tend to differ from the expected value. We compare the variance of three quantities: the total score, the average score, and the normalized total score.

Var(Sn) = nσ²   |   Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n   |   Var(Sn/√n) = σ²

The table shows the variance of these three quantities for increasing numbers of systems (n). For each quantity, we compare the theoretical variance, calculated using mathematical formulas, with the empirical variance, calculated from the simulated attackers. The results show three different behaviors:

  • Total score (Sₙ): Its variance increases with n. As attackers encounter more systems, the differences between their scores become larger.
  • Average score (Sₙ/n): Its variance decreases as n increases. The average scores become more concentrated around the expected value.
  • Normalized total score (Sₙ/√n): Its variance remains constant as n increases. This is important for the Central Limit Theorem.

This experiment demonstrates that the choice of normalization changes the variability of the penetration scores. Dividing by n makes the variance approach zero, while dividing by √n keeps it constant. This distinction is important because it explains why the average score converges to its expected value in the Law of Large Numbers, while the normalized score can retain a non-degenerate distribution, as studied in the Central Limit Theorem.

7. Central Limit Theorem: when the bell curve appears

The Central Limit Theorem describes the distribution of accumulated random fluctuations, not the path of a particular attacker. For any IID model with finite, positive variance, the centered and standardized total (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to N(0,1). In the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the required standardization is exactly Sn/√n: its distribution approaches the standard normal bell curve. At finite n it is still discrete, so the agreement is approximate.

(Sn − nμ) / (σ√n) ⇒ N(0,1)   |   Rademacher: Sn/√n ⇒ N(0,1)

8. Interpretation: systems, attackers, and security

The purpose of this section is to interpret the simulation results in the context of cybersecurity, considering how the number of systems (n) and the number of attackers (m) influence the penetration scores.

The role of the number of systems (n)

Each attacker performs one penetration attempt for each of the n systems.

As n increases, each attacker performs more attempts, and the total score accumulates over a larger number of systems.

  • Bernoulli model: The expected total number of successful penetrations increases with n. However, the average success rate becomes more stable and approaches the probability p, as explained by the Law of Large Numbers.
  • Rademacher model: Positive and negative scores tend to balance each other, so the average score approaches zero as the number of systems increases.

The role of the number of attackers (m)

The number of attackers determines how many independent penetration trajectories are generated.

Increasing m allows us to observe more possible outcomes and construct more representative histograms of the penetration scores.

Although individual attackers may obtain different results, a larger number of simulated attackers makes it easier to identify the statistical behavior predicted by probability theory.

Interpreting the simulation results

The simulation shows that individual penetration outcomes remain unpredictable, even when all attackers follow the same probability model.

However, when the number of systems becomes large, the average scores become more stable, and the normalized scores exhibit predictable statistical patterns.

Conclusion

The experiment demonstrates how probability theory can be used to analyze aggregate penetration scores across multiple systems and attackers.

The model is a simplified representation of cybersecurity attacks because it assumes independent attempts with identical probabilities. Nevertheless, it helps explain how system size and the number of simulated attackers influence the observed statistical results.

9. How to reproduce and discuss the experiment

The purpose of this final section is to summarize the main results obtained from the stochastic penetration simulations and compare them with the theoretical predictions.

Law of Large Numbers

The simulations illustrate that, as the number of systems increases, the average penetration score becomes more concentrated around its expected value.

  • In the Bernoulli model, the average score approaches p.
  • In the Rademacher model, the average score approaches zero.

This behavior is consistent with the Law of Large Numbers, which predicts convergence in probability of the sample average.

Behavior of the Variance

The simulations also illustrate how different normalizations affect the variability of penetration scores.

  • Total score (Sₙ): Its variance increases as n increases.
  • Average score (Sₙ/n): Its variance decreases toward zero.
  • Normalized total score (Sₙ/√n): Its variance remains constant as n increases.

These results explain why the average becomes more stable while the normalized total retains a nonzero level of variability.

Central Limit Theorem

In the Rademacher model, the simulated distribution of the normalized total score becomes increasingly similar to the standard normal distribution as n increases.

This behavior is consistent with the Central Limit Theorem, showing how the sum of many independent random increments can produce an approximately normal distribution after appropriate normalization.

Final Conclusion

The experiments show that, although individual penetration attempts are random, their aggregate behavior follows predictable mathematical laws.

Increasing the number of systems makes the average penetration score more stable, while increasing the number of simulated attackers improves the empirical representation of the score distributions.

Overall, the simulations provide numerical illustrations of the Law of Large Numbers, the behavior of variance under different normalizations, and the Central Limit Theorem.
