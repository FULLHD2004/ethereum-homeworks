Modes of Convergence and Bayesian Inference

A simple, step-by-step explanation of how random quantities "settle down" as data grows (LLN, CLT), why √n appears everywhere, and how Bayes' Theorem lets us update beliefs with data.

Part 1 — Modes of Convergence

Let X₁, X₂, … be random variables (for example, the average of the first n observations). Unlike numbers, random variables can "approach" a limit X in several different ways. Each way answers a different question, and each is used for a different theorem.

One example for all four: an archer shoots arrows one after another. Xₙ is where the n-th arrow lands, X is the bullseye. The archer improves with practice, so we ask: in what sense does Xₙ get closer to X? Each mode below answers with a different kind of "closer".

LOOK AT ONE SHOT AT A TIME

1. In probability (→ᴾ)

P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0

In plain words: pick a large n and a tolerance ε (say 1 cm). The chance that arrow n misses by more than ε is very small, and it gets smaller as n grows.

What it does NOT say: the archer may still make a bad miss now and then, even much later. It only says such misses become rarer and rarer.

LOOK AT THE WHOLE HISTORY

2. Almost surely (→ᵃˢ)

P( Xₙ → X as n → ∞ ) = 1

In plain words: follow one archer through all his shots. With probability 1 there is a moment N after which every arrow lands within ε of the bullseye, forever.

Difference from 1: here bad misses happen only a finite number of times. That is why it is stronger: it controls the entire sequence, not each n separately.

LOOK AT THE AVERAGE PENALTY

3. Mean-square (L²)

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

In plain words: for each arrow, square the distance from the bullseye (so big misses count a lot), then average over all possible archers or repetitions. This average penalty goes to 0.

Why useful: it is easy to compute (it equals variance + bias²). Example: for a sample mean, E[(X̄ₙ − μ)²] = σ²/n → 0.

LOOK ONLY AT THE PATTERN

4. In distribution (→ᵈ)

Fₙ(x) → F(x)

In plain words: forget the bullseye. Look at the cloud of many arrows' landing points: its shape (histogram) gets closer and closer to a given shape F, for example a bell curve.

What it does NOT say: that Xₙ is close to any particular value. Two independent fair coins have the same distribution but are different random variables.

How they relate

Almost surely (2) ⇒ In probability (1) ⇒ In distribution (4)
Mean-square L² (3) ⇒ In probability (1)

The arrow ⇒ means "implies": if the mode on the left holds, the one on the right holds too. The numbers refer to the four cards above.

The reverse implications are false in general. Two useful exceptions: if the limit is a constant, convergence in distribution implies convergence in probability; and L² and a.s. convergence do not imply each other (but neither is stronger in the other direction without extra conditions).

Mode Strength Typical tool to prove it Main use
In probability Medium Chebyshev / Markov inequality Weak LLN, consistency of estimators
Almost sure Strong Borel–Cantelli lemma Strong LLN
L² Strong (moments) Compute E[(Xₙ−X)²] LLN with finite variance, MSE of estimators

Role in the Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent with the same mean μ and variance σ², and let X̄ₙ = (X₁+…+Xₙ)/n be the sample average. The LLN says X̄ₙ approaches μ.

"Gets close" can mean different things, so the LLN comes in three versions, one for each mode of convergence from above:

  • L² version (mean-square): the average squared error of X̄ₙ is E[(X̄ₙ − μ)²] = Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n, which goes to 0 as n grows. So X̄ₙ gets close to μ in mean square.
  • Weak LLN (in probability): for any tolerance ε, the probability that X̄ₙ is farther than ε from μ goes to 0: P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0 (Chebyshev's inequality). It follows directly from the L² version.
  • Strong LLN (almost surely): in your one single experiment, with probability 1 the sequence of averages eventually settles at μ and never leaves it. This is the guarantee that "in the long run, my average will settle at μ". It needs only a finite mean, not a finite variance.

Statistical meaning: the sample mean is a consistent estimator of μ. Consistency of estimators is usually defined through convergence in probability.

Role in the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The LLN says X̄ₙ → μ, but it does not tell us how fast or what the error looks like. The CLT zooms in on the error:

√n ( X̄ₙ − μ ) / σ  →ᵈ  N(0, 1)

This is convergence in distribution: the shape of the standardized error becomes a bell curve, whatever the original distribution (if variance is finite). It cannot be stated with the stronger modes: the standardized quantity does not converge to any fixed random variable, it keeps fluctuating, only its distribution stabilizes.

LLN vs CLT in one sentence: the LLN says the error shrinks to zero (X̄ₙ − μ → 0); the CLT says that, once multiplied by √n, the error has a stable normal shape.

Other important results. The CLT is the starting point. These four tools let us use it in real problems, where things are usually more complicated (for example, σ is unknown).

  • Continuous Mapping Theorem. If Xₙ converges to X (in any of the modes above) and g is a continuous function, then g(Xₙ) converges to g(X). Example: the CLT says √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/σ →ᵈ N(0,1). Squaring it (a continuous operation) gives a quantity that converges in distribution to a χ² with 1 degree of freedom.
  • Slutsky's theorem. If Xₙ →ᵈ X and Yₙ →ᴾ c, where c is a constant, then we can add, multiply and divide them: Xₙ + Yₙ →ᵈ X + c, XₙYₙ →ᵈ cX, Xₙ/Yₙ →ᵈ X/c (c ≠ 0). Example: the CLT uses the true σ, which we do not know. We replace it with the sample standard deviation s, which converges in probability to σ. By Slutsky, √n(X̄ₙ − μ)/s still converges to N(0,1). This is why confidence intervals x̄ ± z·s/√n work.
  • Delta method. If √n(Tₙ − θ) →ᵈ N(0, σ²), then for a smooth function g, √n(g(Tₙ) − g(θ)) →ᵈ N(0, g′(θ)²σ²). In words: if an estimate is approximately normal, any smooth function of it is approximately normal too, and the variance is multiplied by the squared slope g′(θ)². Example: with Tₙ = X̄ₙ and g(x) = x², the variance becomes 4μ²σ².
  • Asymptotic normality of the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE). The MLE θ̂ is the value of the parameter that makes the observed data most probable. For large n, √n(θ̂ − θ) →ᵈ N(0, 1/I(θ)). Here I(θ) is the Fisher information: how much information one observation carries about θ. More information means a smaller variance, so a more precise estimate. Example: for a coin with P(heads) = θ, the MLE is the proportion of heads and I(θ) = 1/(θ(1−θ)), so the variance is θ(1−θ), exactly what the CLT gives.

Why does √n appear everywhere?

It comes from how variance behaves when we add independent random variables.

  1. Variance adds up (needs independence, or at least uncorrelated):
    Var(X₁ + … + Xₙ) = Var(X₁) + … + Var(Xₙ) = nσ²
  2. Typical size of the sum = standard deviation = √(nσ²) = σ√n. The sum grows like √n, not like n. Positive and negative deviations partly cancel.
  3. The average divides by n: Var(X̄ₙ) = nσ²/n² = σ²/n, so the standard deviation is σ/√n. The error shrinks at rate 1/√n.
  4. To keep the error from vanishing or exploding, multiply by exactly √n: Var(√n(X̄ₙ − μ)) = n · σ²/n = σ². With any smaller power it goes to 0 (LLN); with any larger power it blows up. √n is the only "just right" scaling, so the CLT lives there.
Quantity Variance Behavior as n grows
Sₙ = ΣXᵢ nσ² grows
Sₙ / n (average) σ²/n shrinks to 0 (LLN)
Sₙ / √n σ² stays constant (CLT)

Role of independence: if the variables were strongly positively correlated, Var(Sₙ) would grow like n² (extreme case: all copies of the same variable, Var = n²σ²), and averaging would not help at all. In statistics: the standard error σ/√n explains why, to cut the uncertainty of an estimate in half, you need four times more data. Confidence intervals have the form x̄ ± z·s/√n, and standard test statistics contain √n.

Part 2 — Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem and Inference

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),   P(B) > 0

It is the probability of A once we know B has happened: we restrict our attention to the world where B is true and re-scale. In probability theory it is the building block for independence (A and B are independent if P(A|B) = P(A)), the multiplication rule P(A∩B) = P(A|B)P(B), and the law of total probability P(B) = ΣP(B|Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ). In statistics it describes how data depend on parameters, for example P(data | θ).

Bayes' Theorem

Writing P(A∩B) in two ways, P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A), gives:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

For inference, A becomes the unknown parameter θ and B becomes the observed data:

p(θ | data) = p(data | θ) · p(θ) / p(data)   ∝   Likelihood × Prior
BEFORE DATA

Prior p(θ)

What we believe about θ before seeing data: from past studies, expert knowledge, or "no strong opinion".

WHAT DATA SAY

Likelihood p(data|θ)

How probable the observed data are for each possible θ. Seen as a function of θ, it shows which values explain the data best. It is not a probability distribution over θ.

AFTER DATA

Posterior p(θ|data)

Our updated belief: prior knowledge combined with evidence. This is the final answer of Bayesian inference.

NORMALIZER

Evidence p(data)

p(data) = Σ p(data|θ)p(θ) (or an integral). It only rescales so that the posterior sums to 1.

Why is updating so central?

The posterior of today becomes the prior of tomorrow: when new data arrive, we apply Bayes again, and the result is the same as analyzing all data together. This gives a natural way to learn sequentially, to express uncertainty about θ as a full probability distribution (credible intervals, direct statements like "P(θ > 0.5 | data) = 0.9"), to include prior information, and to make decisions.

Example 1 — Medical test (discrete prior)

A disease affects 1% of people (prior). A test is positive for 99% of sick people (sensitivity) and 5% of healthy people (false positives). Someone tests positive. What is P(sick | positive)?

P(S|+) = (0.99 × 0.01) / (0.99 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99) = 0.0099 / 0.0594 ≈ 0.167

Only about 17%, despite a "99% accurate" test. The reason is the low prior: among 10,000 people, about 99 are sick and positive, but about 495 healthy people are also positive. Ignoring the prior (the "base-rate fallacy") would give a completely wrong conclusion.

Example 2 — Estimating a coin's probability of heads (continuous prior)

Let θ = P(heads). Use a Beta(a, b) prior and a Binomial likelihood for k heads in n flips. The posterior is again a Beta, with the simple update rule:

Beta(a, b) + (k heads, n−k tails) ⟹ Beta(a + k, b + n − k),   posterior mean = (a+k)/(a+b+n)
Prior Data Posterior Posterior mean
Flat Beta(1,1) 8 heads in 10 Beta(9, 3) 0.75
Skeptical Beta(20,20) (fair coin) 8 heads in 10 Beta(28, 22) 0.56
Flat Beta(1,1) 80 heads in 100 Beta(81, 21) 0.79
Skeptical Beta(20,20) 80 heads in 100 Beta(100, 40) 0.71

Lesson: with little data (n = 10) the prior strongly changes the conclusion (0.75 vs 0.56). With more data (n = 100) the two posteriors get much closer (0.79 vs 0.71): the data overwhelm the prior. This links back to Part 1: as n grows the likelihood dominates, and Bayesian estimates behave like the maximum likelihood estimate, which is consistent and asymptotically normal.

Advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions

Advantages

  • Uses prior knowledge (useful with small samples)
  • Direct probability statements about parameters
  • Natural sequential updating
  • Works with complex, hierarchical models
  • Credible intervals are easy to interpret

Limitations

  • The prior is partly subjective; different priors give different answers
  • Computation can be hard (the evidence integral), often requiring MCMC
  • A wrong, very strong prior can mislead for a long time
  • The likelihood must be correctly specified

Good practice

  • Use weakly informative priors when knowledge is limited
  • Run a sensitivity analysis with several priors
  • Report prior, likelihood and posterior clearly
  • Collect more data: it reduces prior influence

Summary: the likelihood carries the information in the data; the prior carries what we knew before; the posterior is the balance of the two. With small samples the prior matters; with large samples the likelihood dominates. This closes the circle with Part 1: LLN and CLT describe why, as n grows, estimates become accurate (error ~ 1/√n) and approximately normal, and the same asymptotic behavior governs both classical and Bayesian inference.
