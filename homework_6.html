Contingency Tables and the Chi-Square Test of Independence

How to tell whether two characteristics of a dataset are related: building the table, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected frequencies, the χ² statistic, the p-value, and how to interpret the result.

PART 1

The starting idea

We have a bivariate dataset: for each person or object observed, we record two characteristics. We call them X and F. They are qualitative variables (categories), such as "sport practised" or "sleep quality".

The question is:does knowing the value of X tell us anything about F? If people who exercise regularly sleep better, the two variables seem "linked". If sleep quality is distributed in the same way in every group, they are "independent".

Our example: 230 people, with

  • X = sport practised: None, Occasional, Regular (3 categories);
  • F = sleep quality: Poor, Good (2 categories).

The method has four steps: (1) count the observations in a table, (2) compute what we would expect if the variables were independent, (3) measure the distance between observed and expected with the χ² statistic, (4) decide whether this distance is too large to be just "luck".

PART 2

Contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies

The contingency table counts, for every pair (category of X, category of F), how many observations there are. Each number nij is a joint absolute frequency (row i, column j). You can edit the numbers below: everything else on the page recalculates.

X \ F Poor Good

Table with totals (marginal frequencies)

The marginal frequencies are the totals on the "edge" of the table: summing the rows gives how many observations fall in each category of X (ni·), summing the columns gives the same for F (n·j). The grand total is n. Dividing by n gives the relative marginal frequencies.

Conditional frequencies

Here we "condition": we ask "among those with X = i, what fraction has F = j?". Each cell is divided by the total of its row:

f(F=j | X=i) = nij / ni·

Each row sums to 100%. If all rows were equal to each other (and to the "Total" row), the variables would be independent. Here the rows are quite different: people who do no sport have more poor sleep, people who exercise regularly have more good sleep.

PART 3

Independence: in probability and in statistics

In probability

Two events A and B are independent if knowing that one has occurred does not change the probability of the other:

P(A | B) = P(A)   ⟺   P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)

Example: tossing two coins. The first outcome does not influence the second, so P(heads, heads) = ½ · ½ = ¼.

For categorical variables, X and F are independent if the rule holds for every pair of categories: P(X=i, F=j) = P(X=i) · P(F=j).

In statistics (on data)

In data we do not know the true probabilities, but we can estimate them with relative frequencies: P(X=i) ≈ ni·/n and P(F=j) ≈ n·j/n. If independence held, the expected joint frequency would be the product of the marginals:

expected nij = n · (ni·/n) · (n·j/n) = ni· · n·j / n
Practical translation. "Independence" means the observed table looks like a table with the same row and column totals, but where the values are spread "without preferences", exactly in proportion to the totals.

Expected frequencies in our example

For "None / Poor": 70 · 90 / 230 ≈ 27.39. It means: if sport had nothing to do with sleep, we would expect about 27 people in that cell (we observed 40).

PART 4

The Chi-square (χ²) statistic and the p-value

Now we compare observed (O) and expected (E) in every cell. Large differences = a sign of dependence. So that positive and negative differences do not cancel out, we square them, and we divide by E to "weight" them (being off by 10 on 20 expected is more serious than on 1000):

χ² = ∑i,j (Oij − Eij)² / Eij

Degrees of freedom

With r rows and c columns, once the marginal totals are fixed only some cells are "free" to vary. The degrees of freedom are:

df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

What is the p-value?

If X and F were truly independent, χ² would approximately follow a chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom. The p-value is the probability of obtaining a χ² at least as large as the observed one "by pure chance", assuming independence is true.

  • H0 (null hypothesis): X and F are independent.
  • H1: X and F are associated (dependent).
  • Rule: if p < α (usually α = 0.05) we reject H0; otherwise we lack sufficient evidence to reject it.
Verdict. —

Validity condition: the test works well if the expected frequencies are large enough (rule of thumb: all ≥ 5). Here they are.

How strong is the link? (Cramér's V)

χ² grows with n, so on its own it does not say "how much" the variables are associated. Cramér's index is used, between 0 (no association) and 1 (perfect association):

V = √( χ² / (n · min(r−1, c−1)) )

PART 5

Implementation in Python

The code starts from a "raw" dataset (a list of pairs (x, f)), builds the table and computes everything with the standard library only. The p-value needs scipy (optional).

from collections import Counter

# Raw dataset: one (x, f) pair for each observation
counts = {("None","Poor"):40, ("None","Good"):30,
          ("Occasional","Poor"):30, ("Occasional","Good"):50,
          ("Regular","Poor"):20, ("Regular","Good"):60}
data = [pair for pair, k in counts.items() for _ in range(k)]

# 1) Contingency table and marginals
table = Counter(data)
X = sorted({x for x, _ in data}); F = sorted({f for _, f in data})
n = len(data)
row = {x: sum(table[(x, f)] for f in F) for x in X}   # n_i.
col = {f: sum(table[(x, f)] for x in X) for f in F}   # n_.j

# 2) Conditional frequencies  f(F=j | X=i)
cond = {(x, f): table[(x, f)] / row[x] for x in X for f in F}

# 3) Expected frequencies under independence
exp = {(x, f): row[x] * col[f] / n for x in X for f in F}

# 4) Chi-square and degrees of freedom
chi2 = sum((table[k] - exp[k])**2 / exp[k] for k in exp)
df = (len(X) - 1) * (len(F) - 1)

print(f"chi2 = {chi2:.3f}, df = {df}")   # chi2 = 16.331, df = 2

# 5) OPTIONAL: p-value
from scipy.stats import chi2 as chi2_dist
print("p-value =", chi2_dist.sf(chi2, df))   # about 0.00028

Cross-check: scipy.stats.chi2_contingency(matrix) directly returns χ², p-value, df and the expected table (for tables larger than 2×2 the continuity correction has no effect).

PART 6

Interpretation and conclusions

With the starting data we get χ² ≈ 16.33 with df = 2 and p ≈ 0.0003, far below 0.05. So we reject independence: sport and sleep quality turn out to be statistically dependent (associated). The cells contributing most are "None/Poor" (more cases than expected) and "Regular/Poor" (fewer cases than expected). Cramér's V ≈ 0.27 indicates a moderate association, not a very strong one.

Association is not causation

The test only says that the variables go together more than chance would explain. It does not say why. Possible explanations besides "sport improves sleep":

  • Reverse causation: people who sleep well have more energy to exercise.
  • Confounding variable: for example stress, age or job could influence both sport and sleep.
  • Sample selection: if the data come from a particular group, the link may not hold in general.

To establish a causal link we need controlled experiments (random assignment to groups) or causal inference methods, not the χ² test alone.

Final conclusion. We built the contingency table, computed marginal and conditional frequencies, obtained the expected frequencies under independence and measured the deviation with χ². A large χ² (small p-value) means the data are poorly compatible with independence: there is statistical association. This does not, however, prove that one variable is the cause of the other.

If you edit the numbers in the Part 2 table (for example making the rows almost equal), you will see χ² drop and the p-value rise: the verdict above will change accordingly. The text in this section refers to the initial data.
